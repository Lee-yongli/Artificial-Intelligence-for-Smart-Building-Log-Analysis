EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Smart Facilities SA is the host organisation for this project. The project will investigate the use of artificial
intelligence and machine-learning techniques for analysing smart-building operational data. The publicly
available CU-BEMS Energy Research Data from the Chamchuri 5 building at Chulalongkorn University will
be used as the primary research dataset. The dataset contains minute-level measurements collected
across seven building floors and multiple zones, including air-conditioning, lighting and plug-load power
consumption, together with indoor temperature, relative humidity and illuminance measurements. The
selected dataset is used as a representative development and evaluation dataset for the project prototype
and does not represent the full range of smart-building systems described in the Smart Facilities SA project
environment.

The project will develop and evaluate a reproducible data-analysis pipeline for integrating and analysing
these heterogeneous measurements. The pipeline will support data preparation, data integration, feature
extraction, pattern analysis, anomaly detection, visualisation and evaluation. Python will be used as the

                                                                                                                                       Page 2 of 15
main programming language, together with open-source data science and machine-learning libraries. The
project will compare interpretable statistical baseline methods with several suitable unsupervised
anomaly-detection approaches. This will allow the final recommendations to be based on evidence from
different methods rather than relying on a single model.

The analysis will examine operational patterns at building, floor, zone and device levels where supported
by the available data. The project will investigate potentially unusual patterns such as unexpected changes
in equipment power consumption, unusual temporal behaviour and deviations from typical relationships
between energy consumption and environmental conditions. Because the selected dataset does not
provide comprehensive verified records of equipment faults or abnormal incidents, detected anomalies
will not automatically be treated as confirmed real-world failures. Evaluation will instead use controlled
synthetic anomalies, stability and consistency testing, alert-volume analysis and qualitative review of
representative results.

The main outputs will include a documented data-integration and analysis pipeline, reusable source code,
a canonical analysis-ready dataset or reproducible transformation process, exploratory analysis results,
anomaly-detection results and visualisations. The project will also develop a framework for interpreting
and prioritising detected anomalies based on factors such as magnitude, persistence, recurrence, location
and supporting context. A final technical report will summarise the results, limitations and
recommendations for possible future operational use.

The project is planned over ten teaching weeks. No mandatory software-licensing cost is expected
because the main tools are open source. An optional budget of AUD 200 is included for cloud computing if
local or organisational computing resources are not sufficient. Total student effort is estimated at about
400 hours. This is recorded separately as an estimated labour-value item rather than a direct project cost.

1. PROJECT BACKGROUND AND DESCRIPTION
Smart Facilities SA is the host organisation for this project. Its facilities-management environment includes
a range of smart-building systems, such as access-control devices, heating and cooling systems,
environmental sensors, lifts, power-management systems and building-management servers. The project
focuses on the use of artificial intelligence and machine-learning techniques to support anomaly detection
in smart-building operations.

To provide a realistic and reproducible basis for the research, the project will use publicly available smart-
building operational data collected through the CU-BEMS building energy management system at the
Chamchuri 5 building of Chulalongkorn University. The selected dataset will serve as a representative
development and evaluation dataset for the project prototype and does not represent the full range of
smart-building systems described in the broader Smart Facilities SA project environment.

The selected dataset contains minute-level measurements collected from a seven-storey academic office
building between July 2018 and December 2019. The available data includes power consumption from air-
conditioning, lighting and plug-load systems, together with indoor environmental measurements such as
temperature, relative humidity and illuminance. Measurements are available across multiple floors and
zones, although the number and type of available devices and sensors vary between locations
(Pipattanasomporn et al., 2020).

Analysing this type of smart-building data is not straightforward. Different floors and zones have different
equipment configurations and operating conditions, while normal behaviour can vary according to time,
location, device type and environmental conditions. The data also contains a large volume of minute-level
observations that would be difficult to inspect manually. For these reasons, fixed rules or manual
inspection may not consistently identify unusual operational behaviour. Previous research has examined
data-driven anomaly detection in building and energy environments, where large volumes of sensor and
operational data can be used to identify patterns and potential abnormal behaviour (Himeur et al., 2021).

This project will examine whether artificial intelligence and machine-learning methods can distinguish
normal operational behaviour from unusual patterns in the selected building energy and environmental
data. Potential anomalies may include unexpected changes in equipment power consumption, unusual
temporal patterns, deviations from typical environmental conditions, or inconsistent relationships
between related variables. The project will not assume that one detection method is suitable for all cases.
Instead, interpretable statistical baselines and selected machine-learning approaches will be developed
and compared using the selected dataset. This is necessary because anomaly-detection methods use
different assumptions about normal and abnormal behaviour, and their performance can vary between
datasets and application domains (Chandola et al., 2009).

The project will also consider how detected anomalies can be presented with sufficient context to support
interpretation and prioritisation in a future operational environment. Because the selected dataset does
not provide comprehensive verified records of equipment faults, security incidents or other abnormal
events, a detected anomaly will not automatically be interpreted as a confirmed real-world incident.
Instead, evaluation will use controlled synthetic anomalies, consistency and stability checks, alert-volume
analysis and qualitative review of representative cases.

The project objectives are to:


                                                                                               Page 4 of 15
    •   Create a reproducible workflow for importing, integrating, cleaning, transforming and analysing
        smart-building data across multiple floors and zones.
    •   Develop a consistent data structure for integrating heterogeneous measurements, including
        equipment power consumption and environmental sensor data.
    •   Identify normal temporal, device-level, zone-level and cross-variable operational patterns.
    •   Develop and compare suitable anomaly-detection methods, including statistical baselines and
        selected machine-learning approaches.
    •   Create visualisations that show operational patterns, anomaly timing, affected floors, zones or
        systems, and supporting evidence.
    •   Evaluate the detection methods using controlled synthetic anomalies, consistency and stability
        checks, alert-volume analysis and qualitative review where appropriate.
    •   Develop an interpretation and prioritisation approach that could support facilities or security staff
        in reviewing and triaging detected anomalies in a future operational environment.

The expected benefits for Smart Facilities SA include improved visibility of smart-building operational
patterns and a structured approach for identifying potentially unusual behaviour. The project may help
demonstrate how anomaly-detection methods could support the prioritisation of investigation effort by
facilities or security staff in a future operational environment. The results will also provide evidence about
the usefulness, limitations and practical requirements of AI-assisted monitoring, including the
requirements for extending the prototype to other smart-building or security-related data sources before
any larger-scale production system is considered.



2. PROJECT SCOPE

2.1 Scope Rationale

 • The project scope focuses on the main tasks required for analysing smart-building operational and
    sensor data and is designed to be achievable within the available ten-week project timeframe. It
    covers data integration and preparation, feature engineering, exploratory pattern analysis, anomaly
    detection, evaluation, visualisation and interpretation. These areas are included because the
    performance of anomaly-detection methods depends on the quality, structure and characteristics of
    the available data (Chandola et al., 2009).

 • The project will use the CU-BEMS Energy Research Data from the Chamchuri 5 building as its primary
    research dataset. The analysis will focus on the available measurements across multiple floors and
    zones, including air-conditioning, lighting and plug-load power consumption, indoor temperature,
    relative humidity and illuminance. The selected dataset represents a subset of the broader smart-
    building environment described in the Smart Facilities SA project context. Therefore, the prototype will
    focus on the available CU-BEMS measurements while considering how the approach could be
    extended to other operational or security-related data sources in a future deployment.

 • The project will compare interpretable statistical baseline methods with selected machine-learning
    approaches. It will not assume that one method is suitable for every variable, device or operating
    condition. Visualisation and anomaly prioritisation are also included so that detected patterns can be
    interpreted in their building and operational context and could support facilities or security staff in
    reviewing representative findings in a future operational environment.
                                                                                                Page 5 of 15
 • Production deployment, automated building control, and cybersecurity testing are outside the scope
    of this project. These exclusions keep the project focused on developing and evaluating an analytical
    prototype rather than creating a live operational system.


2.2 Inclusions

 • The following aspects of the project are considered within scope:

 • Review and characterisation of the selected CU-BEMS dataset, including available floors and zones,
    measurement variables, timestamps, sampling frequency, missingness, data volume and quality
    constraints.
 • Data cleaning and preparation, including timestamp processing, duplicate handling, missing-value
   treatment, data-type standardisation and integration of measurements from different floors, zones
   and systems into an analysis-ready structure.
 • Feature engineering at appropriate time, floor, zone and device levels, such as rolling statistics, moving
    averages, rate of change, temporal features, power-consumption patterns and relationships between
    energy consumption and environmental measurements.
 • Exploratory analysis and pattern discovery to identify normal operating patterns, daily and weekly
    cycles, recurring behaviour, differences between floors or zones, and relationships between building
    energy consumption and environmental conditions.
 • Development of interpretable statistical baseline methods and one or more suitable machine-learning
   anomaly-detection approaches for predominantly unlabeled smart-building operational data.
 • Comparison and evaluation of candidate methods using controlled synthetic anomalies, stability and
    consistency testing, alert-volume analysis and qualitative review of representative results.
 • Visualisation of normal operating patterns and detected anomalies, with sufficient contextual
    information to show their timing, location, affected variables and supporting evidence.
 • Development of an interpretation and prioritisation framework based on available evidence, such as
   anomaly magnitude, persistence, recurrence, affected floor or zone and relationships with other
   measurements, to support potential review and triage by facilities or security staff in a future
   operational environment.
 • Documentation, source-code packaging and final presentation of findings, limitations and
   recommendations.


2.3 Exclusions

 • The following aspects of the potential project are explicitly out of scope unless later agreed through
    formal scope control:

 • Deployment of a production, 24/7 monitoring platform within live building-management infrastructure.
 • Direct control of physical building equipment or automated execution of operational responses.
 • Replacement of existing building-management, energy-management, maintenance or monitoring
    systems.
 • Procurement or installation of new sensors, controllers, networking equipment or other building
    hardware.
 • Long-term collection of new operational data beyond the selected research dataset.
 • Formal cybersecurity penetration testing, vulnerability exploitation or forensic investigation.

                                                                                              Page 6 of 15
 • Direct validation of security incidents, access-control events or lift-system anomalies, as these data
   types are not available in the selected CU-BEMS dataset. Their inclusion would require additional
   approved data sources and further development.
 • Confirming that every detected anomaly represents an actual equipment fault or operational failure, as
   the selected dataset does not provide comprehensive verified incident labels.
 • Guaranteeing prediction of all future equipment failures or security incidents; the project is an
     analytical investigation and prototype evaluation.

3. RISKS
The most significant project risks arise from the quality and structure of the selected smart-building
dataset, the absence of verified anomaly labels, the complexity of integrating measurements across floors
and zones, and the limited ten-week project timeframe. These risks may affect both model validity and
project delivery. The project will therefore conduct an early data audit, maintain interpretable baseline
methods, document uncertainty explicitly and avoid dependence on any single modelling technique.

Project success will be judged primarily by reproducibility, technical validity, stability of the anomaly-
detection methods, interpretability of outputs, usefulness of the prioritisation framework and the quality of
evidence supporting conclusions.

Although the chosen CU-BEMS dataset focuses on comprehensive energy and environmental benchmark
records, in the absence of verified on-site equipment fault labels, conventional classification metrics (such
as accuracy, recall, and F1-score) will serve only as auxiliary references rather than definitive standards for
actual deployment performance. Consequently, the evaluation framework will adopt a multi-faceted
approach, including controlled baseline consistency checks, anomaly stability analysis, alert frequency
distribution evaluations, cross-method comparisons, and qualitative reviews of representative cases by
facilities management experts, ensuring the business relevance and interpretability of the prototype
system



7. IMPLEMENTATION PLAN
1. Dataset and project requirements confirmation (Week 1, Milestone M1): Confirm project objectives,
stakeholder expectations, selected CU-BEMS dataset scope, success criteria and technical environment.
Establish the repository, task board and reproducibility conventions.

2. Data audit and analysis structure (Weeks 1–2, M2): Profile the available CU-BEMS files, floors, zones,
variables, timestamps, sampling frequency, data volume, missingness and device configurations. Define
the data structure and mappings required for analysis across selected data sources.

3. Data preparation pipeline (Weeks 2–3, M3): Implement data loading, cleaning, timestamp processing,
duplicate handling, missing-value treatment, standardisation and validation checks. Produce an analysis-
ready dataset or reproducible transformation process.

4. Feature engineering and exploratory pattern discovery (Weeks 3–4, M4): Create temporal, rolling-
statistical, rate-of-change and cross-variable features. Examine daily and weekly cycles, correlations,
differences between floors and zones, recurring operating patterns and relationships between energy
consumption and environmental measurements.

5. Baseline anomaly detection (Weeks 4–5, M5): Implement interpretable statistical baselines such as
thresholds, z-scores, robust scores or rolling-deviation approaches to establish reference behaviour and
identify potential anomalies.

6. Machine-learning anomaly modelling (Weeks 5–6, M6): Implement and compare suitable
unsupervised anomaly-detection methods such as Isolation Forest (Liu et al., 2008), Local Outlier Factor
(Breunig et al., 2000) and other justified techniques. More complex methods will only be included if the
data volume and structure support their use.

7. Evaluation, tuning and robustness analysis (Weeks 6–7, M7): Apply controlled synthetic anomalies,
time-based validation where appropriate, consistency and stability checks and comparison between
representative methods. Tune thresholds and model parameters with attention to alert volume and
interpretability.

8. Visualisation and anomaly prioritisation (Weeks 7-8, M8): Develop dashboard-style views of air-
conditioning, lighting and plug-load power consumption; indoor temperature; relative humidity;
illuminance; and detected anomalies. Support filtering or comparison by time period, floor, zone and
measurement type or system. For representative anomalies, show timing, affected floor or zone,
affected variable or system, magnitude or available anomaly score, duration or persistence,

                                                                                              Page 13 of 15
recurrence and relevant contextual measurements. Use this evidence to support interpretation and
prioritisation.

9. Integrated prototype review (Week 9, M9): Run the end-to-end pipeline on the selected dataset, review
representative outputs, assess technical and usability limitations, and correct high-priority issues before
finalisation.

10. Finalisation and handover (Week 10, M10): Finalise reproducible code, complete documentation and
the technical report, prepare the final presentation or demonstration, document limitations and provide
recommendations for potential future deployment in a live smart-building environment.

11. The schedule assumes that the selected CU-BEMS dataset remains suitable for the planned analysis
after the initial data audit. If significant data-quality issues, missing measurements or structural limitations
are identified, the team will adjust the selected floors, zones, variables, feature set or analytical methods
while maintaining the overall project objective of developing and evaluating a smart-building anomaly-
analysis prototype.
